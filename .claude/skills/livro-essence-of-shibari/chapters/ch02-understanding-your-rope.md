# Chapter 2: Understanding Your Rope

## Core Idea
Rope material, dimensions, and conditioning are not aesthetic choices — they determine control, skin safety, and knot behavior. Jute at 6mm × 7.5–8m is the Japanese standard for a reason; deviations should be deliberate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Material Selection Matrix**: Each fiber type has a specific trade-off profile. Match material to use case, not to preference.
  - When to use: When recommending rope to a beginner or selecting rope for a specific application.
  - How: See reference table below.

- **Rope Treatment Protocol (Hemp/Jute)**: Conditioning rope transforms raw fiber into a consistent, skin-safe tool.
  - When to use: With every new rope and after extended use.
  - How: (1) Burn off loose fibers with a flame; (2) apply oil or wax to the full length; (3) work the rope through your hands or around a post repeatedly to soften; (4) repeat flame + oil as fibers re-emerge with use.

- **Storage and Inspection Rule**: A rope head (the end) showing cracks can no longer bear weight. A body thinned from repeated tension under stress must be retired. Inspect before every session.

## Key Concepts
- **Asanawa**: The traditional Japanese method of twisting rope — the standard construction for kinbaku hemp/jute.
- **Rope head**: The end sections of the rope — the most vulnerable point for wear and the first place to inspect.
- **6mm diameter / 7.5–8m length**: Japanese standard rope spec for kinbaku. Westerners with larger bodies sometimes use 8mm.
- **Jute**: Most commonly used fiber within the Japanese kinbaku community. Rough texture enables friction knots; low elasticity gives the nawashi direct tactile control of tightness.
- **Cotton rope**: Softer than hemp, but elastic — knots tighten under tension and become hard to release. Absorbs oil poorly when dry, becoming abrasive.
- **Nylon rope**: Smooth surface; techniques differ from hemp. Waterproof — safe for wet environments (bath, beach). Widely used in Western bondage.

## Reference Table: Rope Materials

| Material | Texture | Elasticity | Knot Release | Skin Feel | Best Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jute/Hemp | Rough | Low | Easy | Rough, conditions over time | Kinbaku standard |
| Cotton | Soft | High | Difficult under load | Comfortable | Casual, beginner |
| Nylon | Smooth | Medium | Medium | Smooth | Wet environments |
| Manila/Sisal | Very rough | Low | Easy | Harsh | Not recommended |
| Linen | Fine | Low | Easy | Delicate | Aesthetic, advanced |

## Anti-patterns
- **Using Manila or Sisal**: Too rough to soften; not suitable for skin contact in kinbaku.
- **Machine washing rope**: Damages structure; only use washing machine to spin off water after hand-washing.
- **Ignoring rope head condition**: A cracked rope head under suspension load is a failure waiting to happen.
- **Using a rope past its wear threshold**: When the body of the rope has thinned from tension, retire it — never use a structurally compromised rope for anything that bears weight.

## Worked Example
*Treating a new jute rope for first use:*
1. Uncoil the rope and stretch it out.
2. Pass a lighter flame quickly along the length to burn off protruding fibers (do not hold flame — motion only).
3. Apply a small amount of camellia or similar oil to your hands and work it into the rope from one end to the other.
4. Pull the rope back and forth around a smooth post (or between your hands under tension) 30–50 times to work the fibers and distribute the oil.
5. Hang to air dry fully before use.
6. After several sessions, repeat steps 2–4 as the rope will produce new fluff and lose oil with use.

## Key Takeaways
1. Jute is the Japanese kinbaku standard: rough enough for friction knots, inelastic enough for tactile tightness control.
2. Standard spec: 6mm diameter × 7.5–8m length. Prepare several identical ropes.
3. Treatment (flame → oil → work) is not optional — it determines rope behavior, skin safety, and longevity.
4. Inspect rope heads before every session. Crack = retire from load-bearing use.
5. Cotton and nylon each have specific valid use cases; neither replaces jute for traditional kinbaku technique.
