# Chapter 6: Common Kinbaku Ties

## Core Idea
The common ties are the practical vocabulary of kinbaku — each has a specific aesthetic, restriction level, and body interaction. They are not interchangeable: each tie shapes the bottom's body and experience differently. Choose by intent, not by what you happen to know.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Tie Selection by Intent**: Each tie has a dominant characteristic. Match the tie to what you want the session to produce.
  - Erotic/interactive play → Hands Behind the Head (Crotch Rope version)
  - Restriction + stability → Hands Behind the Head (Affixed Upper Arm variation)
  - Body coverage + aesthetic → Tortoise Shell / Rhombus Rope
  - Posture restriction → Futomomo (Thigh Tie)
  - Upper body structure → Pentacle Chest Tie, Arm Binder
  - Suspension-ready → Hip and Crotch Tie (Akechi/Kanna style)

- **Hip and Crotch Tie Variants**: Three versions serve distinct body types and contexts.
  - Simple (Women): lighter structure, accessible entry point.
  - Simple (Men): anatomical adaptation.
  - Akechi/Kanna Style: more complex, suspension-capable, named after its lineage.

- **Futomomo (Thigh Tie) Principle**: Ties the thigh to the calf, restricting leg mobility. Two variations — Letter M (open leg) and Crab Tie — change the bottom's position and exposure significantly.

## Key Concepts
- **Te-age shibari / Koutou ryote shibari**: Other names for the Hands Behind the Head Tie.
- **Pentacle chest tie**: A five-point rope pattern over the chest — primarily aesthetic, with structural support function.
- **Futomomo**: Thigh tie — ankle to thigh, creating a compact folded-leg position.
- **Tortoise Shell Tie (Kame Shibari) / Rhombus Rope**: A full-body diamond rope pattern; the most visually iconic kinbaku style. Built using Lock Stitch crocheting from Ch4.
- **Arm Binder**: Arms bound together behind the back — high restriction, significant shoulder stress. Leads into Strappado.
- **Strappado**: Arms bound behind and pulled upward — high tension on shoulders; requires careful monitoring.
- **Shoulder Carrying Pole Carrier Tie**: Arms over a horizontal structure — controls position and restricts movement by using the body's own structure.
- **Akechi Denki / Nawashi Kanna**: Two of the most influential bakushi in modern kinbaku; their style names specific tie variants in this chapter and Ch7.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using the Affixed Upper Arm variation for lying-down scenes without extra room**: The tie compresses the upper arm and doesn't tolerate long durations without adjustment — leave much more gap if the bottom will be lying down, with palms crossed.
- **Strappado without shoulder assessment**: The shoulder joint is under extreme load. Pre-session flexibility assessment is non-negotiable.
- **Rushing the Tortoise Shell**: The rhombus pattern requires Lock Stitch proficiency from Ch4 — attempting it without that foundation produces uneven pressure.

## Key Takeaways
1. Each common tie has a primary characteristic — match the tie to the intended experience, not to what you practiced last.
2. The Tortoise Shell / Rhombus Rope is the most visually iconic kinbaku tie; it requires Lock Stitch skill from Ch4.
3. The Arm Binder and Strappado carry the highest injury risk in this chapter — shoulder assessment and active monitoring are mandatory.
4. The Akechi/Kanna Style Hip and Crotch Tie is the suspension-ready version — use it when suspension points are involved.
5. Upper arm compression in the Hands Behind Head tie has a time limit — adjust or release before numbness appears.
